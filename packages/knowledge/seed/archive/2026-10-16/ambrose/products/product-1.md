## If I may.

You've taken on a holiday. Perhaps it's your first. Perhaps your mother-in-law is coming, or nine friends and four chairs. Either way, you're holding a plan, and a plan is the only extravagance worth having.

Here is what this plan **is**:

- **A clock.** Twenty-one days of tasks, fifteen minutes each, in the right order. Dates are already filled in for Thanksgiving and Christmas 2026.
- **The sums.** What each plate may cost, what the drinks come to, what the gifts may total, and a place to see where it actually went.
- **The scripts.** The exact words for the invitation, the guest who's late, the uncle who wants to debate, the friend who offers money, and the end of the evening.
- **A table.** Three levels of laying it, from a coffee table to the full estate, with a ruler or your own hand.

And here is what it **is not**:

- **It isn't a cookbook.** Your cooking is your own. I give you the architecture of a menu, not the recipes. Mrs Pryce handled the cooking, and I've never forgotten my place.
- **It isn't a promise of savings.** It gives you a number before you shop, and a way to see the number afterwards. What you do with it is your affair.
- **It isn't etiquette for its own sake.** Every rule in here earns its keep in money, minutes or calm.

Fifteen minutes a day. Start on the day you're on. If you're late, page 7 tells you how to catch up without doing everything at once.

*Very good. Shall we?*

**Ambrose Hale**

## The Daily Fifteen

1. **Find today's T-number.** T-21 means twenty-one days before the gathering. The clock pages (pages 8–11) show today's date beside it.
2. **Do the one task for today.** Set a timer for fifteen minutes. When it rings, stop. If you finish early, you may look at tomorrow's task, but you may not do it. Rest is part of the plan.
3. **Tick the box.** On paper, on the tablet, or in the sheet. The ticks are your record; you'll count them on day T+7.
4. **Note one number.** Each task names one number to write down (a guest count, a cost, a chair count). That's the measurement. It takes ten seconds.

---PAGE--- 6 · HOUSE RULE No. 1 · THE T-21 CLOCK

## House Rule No. 1 · The T-21 Clock

**Start twenty-one days out, at fifteen minutes a day.**

Most hosts try to "get ready" in the last few days, which is why the last few days are miserable. The clock runs backwards from the day instead: each morning you do that morning's job and nothing else. A small, fixed task is easy to start, and nothing piles up for a last-minute push. That's all this is.

### Why twenty-one days

- **Three weeks is three shopping weekends.** Dry goods, drinks and borrowed chairs each get a weekend of their own, instead of one panicked Saturday.
- **Invitations need two weeks.** Guests with children, shifts or travel need fourteen days' notice to say yes properly.
- **Fifteen minutes a day is 5 hours 15 minutes in total.** That's less time than most hosts spend on the last day alone. Spread out, it's never a burden.

### The shape of the clock

| Week | T-days | The work | Your focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Week One: the Decisions** | T-21 to T-15 | Guest list, per-plate number, menu shape, count of what you own | Paper only. No shopping. |
| **Week Two: the Guests and the Gifts** | T-14 to T-8 | Invitations, assignments, gift ceilings, dry goods | Messages and lists |
| **Week Three: the House** | T-7 to T-1 | Guest Brief, borrowing, shopping, laying the table the night before | Hands-on |
| **The Day and After** | T-0 to T+7 | The sweep, the day, the thank-yous, the Reckoning | Enjoy it. Then measure it. |

### The two calendars inside this plan

- **Thanksgiving clock:** T-21 is **Thu 5 Nov 2026**; the day is **Thu 26 Nov**.
- **Christmas clock:** T-21 is **Fri 4 Dec 2026**; the day is **Fri 25 Dec**.
- **Friendsgiving or anything else:** use the blank clock on page 11 and write your own dates.

**Hosting both?** Run them in turn. The Thanksgiving clock ends with its Reckoning on Thu 3 Dec, and the Christmas clock has already begun on Fri 4 Dec. What you learn from one goes straight into the other.

## House Rule No. 2 · The Per-Plate Number

**Set the budget per plate before you choose the menu.**

Most hosts choose the menu, then discover the cost at the till. Do it the other way round: the number first, then the menu that fits inside it. A cost you choose in advance can be planned around; a cost you discover at the till can only be paid. The number isn't a punishment. It's a frame, and a frame is what makes a picture.

### The calculator

| Line | Gathering 1 | Gathering 2 |
|---|---|---|
| A · Total I'm willing to spend on food, drink and the table | $______ | $______ |
| B · Guests, **host included** | ____ | ____ |
| **C · Per-Plate Number (A ÷ B)** | **$______** | **$______** |
| D · Dishes guests are bringing (from page 14); these cost you nothing | ____ | ____ |

### Where the plate goes (a starting split, not a law)

Multiply your total (line A) by each percentage. Move the percentages to suit your menu, as long as they add up to 100.

### A worked example (illustrative)

Ten people, host included, and a total of $150. **Per plate: $15.** Showpiece $52.50 · sides and bread $37.50 · nibbles $15 · pudding $15 · drinks $22.50 · table $7.50. Two guests bring a side each and one brings the pudding, so the host's sides line halves and the pudding line comes back to the purse, to spend or not as the host likes.

*These figures are an illustration of the arithmetic, not a prediction of what your gathering will cost. Prices vary by place and season.*

**The test:** if the menu you want doesn't fit the number, change the menu, not the number. Page 27 shows three menu structures that fit most numbers.

## House Rule No. 4 · Assign Dishes, Not Dollars

**Never send a guest an invoice.**

Asking guests for money turns them into customers, and customers judge the service. Asking a guest for one specific dish makes them part of the house. If the budget is tight, change the format (a potluck, a brunch, fewer guests) and say so in the invitation, plainly and early.

**Good jobs to assign:** a side that travels cold · bread and butter · the cheese · the pudding (dessert) · ice and the soft drinks · the flowers or greenery · the after-dinner game · "arrive fifteen minutes early and do the coats". **Never assign:** the showpiece, or anything that needs your oven in the last hour.

### Scripts

**Assigning a dish:**
> "We'd love you to bring one thing: [the green beans, for ten]. Something that travels and can sit at room temperature is perfect. Everything else is handled."

**Changing the format honestly (budget is tight):**
> "This year it's a proper potluck: I'm doing the [turkey/main] and the table, and everyone brings one dish. I'll send you yours by Friday."

**The guest who offers money:**
> "That's very kind, and no. If you'd like to help, would you bring [the ice and the sparkling water]?"

**The guest who insists on money:**
> "Then let's call it the wine fund for next time, and next time is at yours." *(Smile; accept nothing.)*

**The guest who brings something else entirely:**
> "How kind. It's going straight on the table." *(It goes on the table. Never mention the assigned dish.)*

### The order of laying (the quickest route round a table)

☐ 1. Cloth or runner, pressed (or a bare wooden table, wiped: equally proper)
☐ 2. All the plates, measured from the edge

### Glass order

The water glass sits just above the knife tip. Wine glasses go to its right, in the order they'll be used, either in a straight line or a short diagonal stepping down towards the guest. The glass used first is always the one furthest from the plate.

## Three Napkin Folds (three folds each, never thirty)

*Press the napkins first: a pressed napkin in a plain fold beats a creased one in a swan.*

**1 · The Oblong** (any table, any napkin)
☐ Fold in half, top to bottom.
☐ Fold in half again, left to right.
☐ Fold in thirds lengthwise. Lay it to the left of the forks, or across the plate.

**2 · The Pocket** (holds the cutlery: buffets and Kitchen Suppers)
☐ Fold into quarters; turn so the open corners point to the top right.
☐ Fold the top layer down diagonally to make a band.
☐ Turn it over, fold the sides under, turn it back, and slide the knife and fork into the band.

**3 · The Plain Triangle** (Proper Dinner and Full Estate)
☐ Fold in half corner to corner to make a triangle.
☐ Fold the two side corners up to the top point to make a diamond.
☐ Fold the diamond in half beneath itself; stand it on the plate.

**Paper napkins** are perfectly proper at a Kitchen Supper. Use the Oblong; it hides the print.

## Candle Rules

| Rule | Why |
|---|---|
| **Unscented on the dining table** | Scent competes with the food. Scented candles belong in the hall or the loo (bathroom). |
| **Below eye level, or well above it** | Guests must see each other across the table. Short candles or tall thin tapers; nothing in between. |
| **Lit before the doorbell** | Lighting candles while guests take their coats looks like you've been caught. |
| **Clear of greenery, napkins and sleeves** | Leave a hand's width of clear table around every flame; use a holder that sits steady. |
| **Never left burning in an empty room** | Snuff them when the table is cleared, and before anyone goes to bed. |
| **Trim wicks to about ¼ inch (6 mm) the night before** | A short wick burns cleaner and steadier. |

## Scripts · Keeping the Table Pleasant

### "The table is not a debate" (politics, religion, anything that starts to heat)

**The house rule, said once, lightly, at the start:**
> "One house rule: no politics at table. I'll happily hear it all over the washing-up."

**When it starts anyway:**
> "I'm going to hold us to the house rule. Now, who's had the best meal of their year? I want specifics."

**When it won't stop:**
> "[Name], would you help me with the pudding?" *(Take them to the kitchen. Give them a job. Return with the pudding.)*

> "Thank you, I'll take that into account next year." *(Then a task:)* "Would you carve? You've a steadier hand than me."

### The guest who asks what it all cost

> "Less than you'd think, and more than I'll say." *(Smile. Change the subject. A host leaves no fingerprints: guests should never feel the cost.)*

Accept, with one specific job: *"Would you fill the water jugs?"* A vague "oh, would you?" puts them in your way. A specific job puts them at ease.

> "There's a basket by the door if anyone would like a night off from theirs." *(An offer, never an order.)*

### The guest who's driving, or has had enough

> "I've made a pot of coffee, and there's sparkling elderflower in the fridge. And the sofa's yours if you'd rather stay."

Plan the non-alcoholic drink with the same care as the wine, and put it first on the drinks table. No drink is ever pressed on anyone.

> "That glass has been waiting years for its moment." *(Cloth, dustpan, fresh glass. Never let the guest clear it up.)*

## Drinks Maths

*A planning sum for buying, not a target for drinking. Never press a drink on anyone.*

**The bottle:** a 750 ml bottle of wine pours about **five glasses** of 150 ml (5 oz).
**The planning rule of thumb:** allow about **two drinks per guest for the first hour, then one per guest for each hour after.**

| Line | Sum | Mine |
|---|---|---|
| A · Guests who'll drink alcohol | count | ____ |
| B · Hours (arrival to close) | from page 5 | ____ |
| C · Drinks per drinking guest | 2 + (B − 1) | ____ |
| D · Total alcoholic drinks | A × C | ____ |
| E · Bottles of wine, if all wine | D ÷ 5, rounded up | ____ |
| F · Guests not drinking alcohol | count | ____ |

## Briefing 1 · The Clock (listen on T-21) · about 3 minutes 20 seconds

> Good morning. Ambrose Hale. … Twenty-one days.
>
> That's where we start, and I'd like you to know what the next three weeks will ask of you. Fifteen minutes a day. Not an hour. Not a weekend lost to lists. Fifteen minutes, every day, in the right order.
>
> The clock has three weeks in it. The first week is decisions: who's coming, what each plate may cost, what you already own, and the one dish that's allowed to be difficult. You'll buy nothing in week one. That's deliberate. The decisions are what make the shopping short.
>
> The second week is guests and gifts: the invitation, the assignments, the ceilings. The third week is the house: borrowing chairs, clearing the coat space, and laying the table the night before. Then the day itself, and after it, the thank-yous and a short reckoning.
>
> Today's task is the first page that matters: Your Gathering at a Glance, page five. Write the occasion, the date, the start time … and, if I may insist, the end time. An evening without an end time is a kindness to nobody. Then the guest list. Count yourself in it. Hosts always forget the host.
>
> At the bottom of page five there's a line for three words: how you want this to feel. Calm, warm, generous … whatever's true for you. Write them in ink. On the night before, you'll read them again, and any task that doesn't serve them may be dropped without guilt.
>
> Two small habits, and then I'll leave you to it. First: when the timer rings, stop. You may look at tomorrow's task; you may not do it. Rest is part of the plan. Second: every task asks you to write down one number. A guest count, a cost, a chair count. It takes ten seconds, and on the last day you'll see exactly where your three weeks went.
>
> If you've opened this late, and many do, turn to page seven: the Late-Start Rescue. You don't do the missed days. You do the missed decisions, in one sitting, and then you join the clock where it is today.
>
> That's all for now. Page five, the guest list, the three words. Fifteen minutes. … Very good. I'll speak to you again on day fourteen.

## Briefing 2 · The Sums (listen on T-14) · about 3 minutes 30 seconds

> Fourteen days. … Good morning. Today the invitations go out, and today we look at the sums.
>
> First, your Per-Plate Number, page twelve. Have a look at it now. If the guest list has grown since you wrote it, divide again. The total doesn't move; the number per plate does. That's the whole discipline. If the menu you'd like doesn't fit the number, change the menu, not the number.
>
> Second: assign dishes, not dollars. Tomorrow you'll fill in the Assignment Sheet on page fourteen. Every guest who offers to help gets one specific job: the beans, for ten; the ice and the sparkling water; the pudding. Never money. The moment you ask a guest to pay, they become a customer, and customers judge the service. A guest with a dish becomes part of the house.
>
> If money is tight this year, there's no shame in it. Change the format instead: a potluck, a brunch, fewer guests. And say so plainly in the invitation. People are generous when they're told the truth early.
>
> Third, the gifts, if this is Christmas. You've set a season total and a ceiling for every name. Keep them in ink. When something costs more than its ceiling, the difference comes from another name that same week, or you choose again. The total holds. And every idea passes the Three-Gift Test: use it, eat it, or keep it ten years.
>
> Fourth, and this one matters to me: the Staff and Service list. The cleaner, the sitter, the dog walker, the mail carrier, the bin collectors. Everyone who serves you by hand all year is thanked by the fifteenth of December. If the budget won't stretch, lower the sum. Never skip the card.
>
> Finally, one habit for the rest of the plan: the Spend Log, page thirteen. Every receipt goes on it the same evening. One minute. It's not there to scold you. It's there so that next year's number is a real one, and not a hopeful guess.
>
> Now: the invitation. The script is on page twenty-two. Send them all at once, ask for a reply by day nine, and ask about anything people don't or can't eat. … Fifteen minutes. Very good. Now, about your spending.

## Briefing 3 · The Guests (listen on T-7) · about 3 minutes 30 seconds

> Seven days. … Good morning. This is the week the house gets its hands dirty, so today we prepare the guests.
>
> First, write the Guest Brief, page twenty-two. Six lines: time, door, dress, parking, what to bring, and when it ends. You'll send it on day two, forty-eight hours out. Today you send something shorter: the one-week note. "One week to go. I'll send the details on Tuesday." That's all. A guest who knows what's coming relaxes before they arrive, and a relaxed guest is half the evening's work done for you.
>
> Second, if your showpiece is frozen poultry, do the thaw sum on page twenty-nine today. A large bird needs days in the fridge, not hours, and the sum tells you the date it must go in. Clear the shelf for it now. The figures on that page come from the regulators, the U S D A and the Food Standards Agency, and if they ever differ from the regulator's own page, the regulator is right.
>
> Third, the scripts. Read pages twenty-three and twenty-four once, today, so the words are in your mouth before you need them. Three to remember.
>
> The late guest: the table waits fifteen minutes, never longer. Then a text: "No rush. We're sitting down and I'm keeping you a plate." And when they arrive, you never mention the time again.
>
> The uncle with opinions: "One house rule: no politics at table. I'll happily hear it all over the washing-up." Said once, lightly, at the start. If it begins anyway, ask him to help you with the pudding.
>
> And the guest who asks what it all cost: "Less than you'd think, and more than I'll say." Then change the subject. A good host leaves no fingerprints. Guests should never feel the bill.
>
> Tomorrow you borrow: chairs, glasses, serving dishes. Borrow chairs, not money. Put a strip of masking tape with the lender's name under everything you borrow, and it'll all go home to the right house.
>
> And the drinks. Put the non-alcoholic option first on the table, chosen with the same care as the wine. Nobody is ever pressed to drink. … Fifteen minutes. Very good. I'll speak to you again the night before.

## Briefing 4 · The Night Before (listen on T-1) · about 3 minutes

> The night before. … Good evening.
>
> Tonight there are sixteen ticks on page thirty-three, and when they're done, tomorrow you'll be a guest at your own party. Let's go through the ones that matter most.
>
> The table first. Lay it at the level you chose, every plate one inch from the edge. A ruler if you have one; the top joint of your thumb if you don't. One kind of thing at a time, all the way round: plates, then forks, then knives, then glasses. Then stand at the head of the table and look down each side. Straighten whatever catches your eye.
>
> Then the serving dishes. Every one comes out tonight, and inside each one goes a card with the name of what it will hold. Six dishes, six cards. Nothing forgotten in the fridge, and nobody asking you where the gravy goes while you're carving.
>
> Drinks chilling. Ice in the freezer. The coat space cleared. Bins emptied, with a spare liner in the bottom. The dishwasher emptied, so it can take the first load. The oven timetable stuck on the fridge.
>
> Now the sixteenth tick, which most hosts skip: your own place. A chair and a plate for you, at your own table. You're going to sit down tomorrow. That is not optional.
>
> Tomorrow, ten minutes before the doorbell, you'll do the Sweep on page thirty-five. Ten stops, a minute each. The last stop is a glass of water, drunk, with your shoulders down and your apron off.
>
> And when everyone's seated, one sentence of welcome. Something like: "Thank you all for coming. There's more than enough, the cook has stopped worrying, and nobody needs to be anywhere until eight. Do start."
>
> Before you go up, read your three words on page five. Whatever isn't done tonight, and doesn't serve those words, can stay undone.
>
> Then bed, at a respectable hour. … The house is ready because you made it ready, fifteen minutes at a time. Very good. Good night.

## Briefing 5 · After (listen on T+2) · about 3 minutes

> Two days after. … Good morning. I hope you sat down.
>
> Three things this week, and then the holidays are properly closed.
>
> The first is the thank-you notes, page thirty-seven. Anyone who brought a dish, lent a chair, helped with the washing-up or hosted you: a note each. Four lines. The thing, one specific detail, the person, and your name. "Thank you for the beans. They went in minutes, and Gran asked for the recipe." That detail is the whole note. Without it, it's a form letter.
>
> If it's Christmas, you've until the second of January. Do one a day with your morning tea, from the twenty-seventh, and they're done without a single evening lost to them. And if one's late, send it anyway. "Late, but no less grateful." A late note is still a note.
>
> The second is the borrowed things. Washed, dried, and back to their owners within two days, each with a short note. A house that returns things promptly is always lent to again.
>
> The third is the Reckoning, on day seven, page thirty-nine. Fifteen minutes with the Spend Log and the clock. What did each plate actually cost? How many ticks did you make? Where did your calm score dip? And which dishes came back to the kitchen untouched? That last one is the most honest feedback a host ever gets.
>
> Then write three things to keep, three to change, and one to drop entirely. Put next year's starting number in the sheet, and the sheet will build next year's clock for you. That's the loop: plan, do, log, reckon, adjust. It means next year's gathering starts from what you learned, not from a guess.
>
> If anything went wrong, turn to page forty, When You Slip. The showpiece is the one dish allowed to fail; that's why there's only one. Nobody remembers the chairs. They remember being welcome.
>
> And if you're hosting Christmas too, the Christmas clock starts on the fourth of December, and you start it knowing exactly what you'd change. … Very good. Now, about your spending.
