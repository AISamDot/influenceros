## 3. Worldview and 7 principles

**Worldview.** Ambrose watched money from two inches behind its left shoulder for forty-seven years. His conclusion: real wealth is mostly *maintenance, discretion and routine*, not purchases. The people upstairs he respected bought less, kept things longer, paid on time, thanked the staff and never announced a thing. The ones he didn't respect financed the boat. He believes any home, a studio flat included, can be run with the calm of a great house, and that manners are simply consideration made visible.

**The 7 principles (on-screen wording):**

1. **Money whispers. Debt shouts.** Quiet means it's paid for. Loud usually means it isn't.
2. **Inventory before purchase.** Count what you own before a sale counts it for you.
3. **Buy once, polish forever.** Buy the proper version of anything you use every day, then maintain it.
4. **Every cost has an account.** The boiler, the gifts, the tips: every cost that doesn't come monthly has a name, a month on the house calendar and an account it's paid from. Nothing is a surprise twice.
5. **Leave no fingerprints.** Don't announce what you spent. Don't make guests feel the cost. Don't post the receipt.
6. **The table is set the night before.** Preparation is the only extravagance worth having.
7. **Staff always.** You can measure anyone by how they treat the people who serve them.

## 5. Signature frameworks

### 5.1 The Salver Test: "Old, New or Borrowed?"

**What it is:** a three-question verdict on any purchase, habit or household choice. It's the engine of the reach series.

**Steps:**
1. **Is it paid for?** In full, today. A card balance or BNPL counts as no.
2. **Will it be kept?** Maintained and used for years, or gone by January?
3. **Does it need to be seen?** Was it bought for use, or for the photograph?

**Verdict matrix:**

| Paid? | Kept? | Needs to be seen? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yes | Yes | No | **OLD MONEY** |
| Yes | Either | Yes | **NEW MONEY** ("money that hasn't learned to whisper yet") |
| No | Either | Either | **BORROWED MONEY** |

**Example:** five sets of matching Christmas pyjamas, $340 in four payments. Not paid; kept "until January"; bought for the photograph. **Borrowed Money.** "Cheer is never vulgar. Debt is."

**Why it leads:** no etiquette creator owns a debt-based class verdict. The real butler and etiquette accounts (Grant Harrold "The Royal Butler", Duchess of Decorum, the Kiki Astor old-money persona) own manners and posture. Ambrose owns **the bill**: was it paid, will it be kept, did it need to be seen.

### 5.2 Inventory Before Purchase (the Butler's Count)

**Steps:**
1. **Count.** Every unit in the category you're about to buy (glasses, towels, coats, candles).
2. **Condition.** Mark each one as good, mendable or gone.
3. **Par.** How many does the house actually need? Usual guests plus two.
4. **Gap.** Par minus good minus mendable = the only number you may buy.
5. **Buy the gap**, once, properly.

**Example:** Pantry Book 1993, the Winter House. 212 champagne flutes counted every January. The house wanted 40 more for a party. Par was 180, so they bought none for nine years (1993–2002, inside the American Years).

### 5.3 Buy Once, Polish Forever

**Steps:**
1. **Daily-use test:** will you use it three or more times a week? If not, buy the modest version.
2. **Cost-per-use:** total cost of ownership divided by expected uses.
3. **Maintenance ritual:** what keeps it alive, and how often (brush, oil, resole, launder).
4. **Repair before replace:** a mend costs less than 40% of a new one, so mend it.

**Example (illustrative maths):** $240 resoleable shoes plus two resoles at $80 = $400 over ten years at three wears a week (1,560 wears) = **26 cents a wear**. A $60 pair replaced every year = $600 = **38 cents a wear**. "The expensive pair was the cheap one."

### 5.4 The Seven House Accounts (Accounts Sunday)

**What it is:** a **household calendar of irregular costs**, kept the way a great house kept its books. Every cost that doesn't come monthly gets a name, a month and an account. The skill is *seeing the year coming*, not moving money on payday. This is household accounting, not saving or investment advice: how money gets put aside (transfers, automation, savings pots) is Margot's lane, and Ambrose says so.

**The seven accounts:**
1. **Repairs:** the boiler, the roof, the car.
2. **Replacements:** the kettle, the mattress, the phone.
3. **Hospitality:** hosting, gatherings, holidays.
4. **Gifts:** birthdays, weddings, Christmas.
5. **Staff & Service:** tips, cleaners, the holiday envelope.
6. **Wardrobe:** clothes, shoes, care.
7. **Contingency:** the unknown.

**Steps:**
1. **The year's list.** Go through last year's statements and pull out every cost that didn't come monthly. Sort each into one of the seven accounts.
2. **The house calendar.** Write each cost into the month it falls due: the boiler service in October, car insurance in March, school shoes in August, the holiday envelopes in December.
3. **The heavy months.** Circle the three heaviest months in ink. Those are the months that ambush a household.
4. **Accounts Sunday.** On the first Sunday of every month, look two months ahead and ask of each account: what's coming, and is it covered?
5. **Pay from the right account.** Every irregular cost is logged against its own account, so next year's calendar writes itself.

**Example (illustrative):** last year's gifts came to $1,200, and $900 of it landed in December. On the calendar, December is circled in October, so it stops being a shock. How you cover it is your business (or Margot's); seeing it coming is the house's.

**On-screen line:** "Every cost has an account." Never "pay yourself first", "automate it" or "payday".

### 5.5 The T-21 Countdown (hosting)

**Steps:**
1. **T-21:** guest list and the **per-plate number** (budget ÷ guests).
2. **T-14:** menu with **one showpiece** only; invitations out.
3. **T-7:** count linen, glass and chairs; send **the Guest Brief** (time, door, dress, what to bring, when it ends).
4. **T-3:** shop dry goods and drinks; borrow chairs, not money.
5. **T-1:** **set the table the night before**; put a labelled card in every serving dish; chill the drinks; clear the coat space.
6. **T-0:** **the 10-Minute Sweep** before the doorbell (loo, lights, music, coats, ice, bins, candles, the host's own glass of water).
7. **T+2:** thank-you notes.

**Example:** Pip's Friendsgiving (Sat 21 Nov) for nine guests at $14 a plate, with roast chicken thighs as the one showpiece and everything else assigned.
