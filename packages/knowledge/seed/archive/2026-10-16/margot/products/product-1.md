## Page 6 · The Five Rules (1–3)

These are the rules underneath every script. If you forget the words in the room, remember the rules.

### Rule 1. Receipts Before Requests
**Three numbered wins before any ask.**
A manager has to justify your raise to someone else: their boss, HR, a budget spreadsheet. Your receipts are the sentences they'll copy into that justification. Make it easy for them.
- *Looks like:* "I took over the supplier contracts in March. We cut late deliveries from 14 a month to 3."
- *Doesn't look like:* "I've worked really hard this year."
- **Check:** each receipt has a number, a before and after, or a named thing you now own.

### Rule 2. Number, Then Hush
**Say it once. Then count to ten.**
The silence after a number feels endless. It's usually about four seconds. Whoever fills it first tends to soften it, so let it be them. Your job is to say a researched number clearly and then let it sit.
- *Looks like:* "I'm asking to move to [number]." *(one, two, three… ten)*
- *Doesn't look like:* "I'm asking for [number], but obviously I know things are tight, so even something closer to…"
- **Check:** you said one number, not a range, and you didn't explain it twice.

### Rule 3. No Is a Date
**Turn "not now" into "what, and by when".**
"No" often means "not yet" or "not like this". Your follow-up question turns it into conditions and a date you can both write down.
- *Looks like:* "Understood. What would need to be true for this to happen, and by when?"
- *Doesn't look like:* "Oh, okay, no worries."
- **Check:** you leave with at least one condition and one date, or a clear "no plan", which is information too.

## Page 7 · The Five Rules (4–5)

### Rule 4. Ask in Writing Twice
**Request the meeting in writing. Confirm what was agreed in writing.**
Memory is generous to whoever's holding the budget. The first email makes the meeting real; the second makes the outcome real. It isn't confrontational. It's admin, and admin is how things actually happen.
- *Looks like:* "Thanks for today. To confirm what we agreed: …"
- **Check:** two emails exist, one before, one within 24 hours after.

### Rule 5. Never Apologise for the Ask
**"Sorry is for stepping on feet."**
Asking about your pay is a normal part of having a job. Apologising frames it as an imposition and invites a smaller answer.
- *Swap:* "Sorry, I know it's a bad time, but…" → "Thanks for making time. I'd like to talk about my pay."
- *Swap:* "I hate to ask, but…" → "I'd like to ask…"
- *Swap:* "Sorry, is that too much?" → *(hush)*
- **Check:** no "sorry", "just", "I hate to ask" or "I know it's a lot" in your script. Circle them if you find them.

## Page 9 · The Receipts Sheet (1): digging

**Rule 1: Receipts Before Requests.** First you dig. Don't judge anything yet; write it all down. Twenty minutes, timer on.

**Where to look:** your sent email, your calendar, project boards, chat threads where someone said "thank you", last year's goals, your performance review, customer feedback, anything you trained someone on.

### The four prompt families

**Money (made, saved or protected)**
- What did I do that brought money in, or stopped money going out?
- Did I find a cheaper supplier, cut waste, catch an error, renegotiate anything?
- Did I help win, keep or grow a client or account?

**Time (saved for me, the team or customers)**
- What used to take longer before I changed it?
- What did I automate, template, simplify or fix for good?
- Whose job got easier because of something I built?

**Scope (more than the job I was hired for)**
- What do I now do that isn't in my job description?
- Whose work did I cover, and for how long?
- What did I take on when someone left?

**People (trained, helped, kept)**
- Who did I train, onboard or mentor?
- Who would say I made their year easier?
- Did I fix a relationship with a team, client or partner?

## Page 14 · Why you have to ask (and the Cost of Silence sum)

### The 2027 picture, briefly (reported, Oct 2026)
- **US:** employer surveys project 2027 pay budgets at roughly 3.3–3.6% in total. Only about a third of US firms plan across-the-board raises, down from the year before. Most of the pot goes to merit increases, which means someone decides who gets more.
- **UK:** the median pay award is expected to be around 3%.
- **What it means:** the default raise is small, and the extra goes to people whose managers have a reason (and a written case) to give it to them. Asking is how you become one of those people.

*Sources: Marsh/Mercer, Payscale, WTW, WorldatWork and Korn Ferry via PlanSponsor and HR Dive; Brightmine (UK). See page 44. These are averages across employers, not a prediction for yours.*

### The Cost of Silence sum
How much does not asking cost? Do the simple version. No growth, no investing, just the money.

| Line | Example | Yours |
|---|---|---|
| A. The extra you'd get each month, after tax, if the ask worked | 200 | |
| B. × 12 months | 2,400 | |
| C. × years you might stay at roughly this pay level, plus future raises built on top | 5 | |
| **D. Cost of silence (B × C)** | **12,000** | |

`[FRANK]` *Illustrative. A simple sum: no growth, no later raises compounding on it, no tax changes. Not a prediction, and not a promise that the ask works.*

**Why it's bigger than it looks:** most future raises are a percentage of your pay, so a higher base usually carries into every later raise and into anything calculated from your pay (like some workplace pension or retirement contributions). The sum above leaves all of that out, so treat D as a rough floor for what the ask is worth, not a forecast of what you'll get.

## Page 15 · Rehearse: Kettle, Cat, Mirror

**Rung 3.** Your number has to sound normal in your mouth before it can sound normal in the room. The first time you say it out loud should not be in front of your manager.

### The drill
1. **The kettle.** Say your number to the kettle while it boils. Just the sentence: "I'm asking to move to [number]."
2. **The cat** (or the dog, the plant, the radiator). Say your opening plus the number.
3. **The mirror.** Full script b, out loud, then the 10-second hush, looking yourself in the eye. Don't smile it away.
4. **The audios.** Play the six rehearsal audios (page 39). Margot reads the manager's line; you answer out loud in the gap.
5. **The full run.** Start to finish, out loud, timed. Aim for under 3 minutes of you talking.

## Page 18 · Script b: The in-person ask, number first

**Use when:** you're in the pay meeting (in person or on a call).

| They say | You say |
|---|---|

## Page 19 · Script c: "There's no budget"

**Use when:** they say there's no money, the budget's spent, or "my hands are tied".
**Rule:** 3. No Is a Date.

**Say**
> "Understood. What would need to be true for this to happen, and by when?"
>
> ⏸ **Hush.**

**Then, depending on the answer:**

| They say | You say |
|---|---|
| "Next budget cycle." | "Great. When does that get set? Could we meet two weeks before that, and can we agree now what I'd need to show by then?" |
| "If you hit [target]." | "Let's write that down. If I hit [target] by [date], we'd move to [number]. Can you confirm that in an email after this?" |
| "I don't know." | "That's fair. Could you find out what the options are and come back to me by [date]?" |
| "Nothing, really." | "Thanks for being straight with me. In the meantime, could we look at [non-salary ask: title, training budget, an extra day's leave, remote days]?" (Script h.) |

**Then:** script m (confirm in writing) the same day.

**Don't say:** "Oh, no worries." It's worry-shaped. It's okay to have worries.

**Why it works:** "No budget" is often "not yet" or "not without a reason I can take upstairs". Asking for conditions turns a closed door into a list, and a date turns a list into a plan.

## Page 21 · Script e: Countering a low number

**Use when:** they come back with an amount below your number (and possibly below your floor).
**Rules:** 2 and 5.

**Say**
> "Thank you, I appreciate that. Based on [range data] and [receipt], I was looking for [number]. Is there flexibility to get closer to that?"
>
> ⏸ **Hush.**

**If they say… → You say…**

| They say | You say |
|---|---|
| "This is the most I can do." | "Thanks for being clear. If that's the number for now, could we agree a review date in [3–6] months to get to [number], and what I'd need to show?" |

## Page 25 · Script i: Cost-of-living vs merit

**Use when:** your company gives a standard "cost-of-living" or across-the-board increase, and you want to talk about merit on top. Or they treat the standard raise as your raise.

**Say**
> "Thanks for the [X]% increase; I know that's going to everyone. I'd like to talk separately about my pay for the work I've done this year: [receipt 1] and [receipt 2]. Based on [range data], I'm asking to move to [number]."
>
> ⏸ **Hush.**

**If they say… → You say…**

| They say | You say |
|---|---|
| "That *was* your raise." | "I understand that's the company-wide increase. I'm asking about a merit increase for my role specifically. How are merit increases decided here?" |
| "Everyone's getting the same." | "Understood. Is there a separate process for people whose role has grown? I'd like to be considered for it." |
| "Merit's already allocated." | Script c: "What would need to be true, and by when?" |

**Why it matters:** a standard increase keeps pay roughly in step with rising prices (sometimes not even that). It doesn't recognise what you did. Those are two different conversations, and you're allowed to have both.

**Don't say:** "Prices have gone up so much." It's true for everyone, so it isn't a reason for you specifically. Your receipts are.

## Page 28 · Script l: Finding out a peer earns more

**Use when:** you learn that someone in the same or a similar role is paid more (a new hire, a posted range, a conversation).
**First, breathe.** Don't go into the meeting the same day you found out. Do the Range work first.

**Know your rights, roughly (check your own country):**
- **US:** the National Labor Relations Act generally protects most private-sector, non-supervisory employees who discuss pay with colleagues.
- **UK:** the Equality Act 2010 protects conversations about pay aimed at finding out whether there's pay discrimination, whatever your contract says.
- **Elsewhere:** check your national labour or equality body.

**Say**
> "I'd like to talk about my pay. I've looked at [posted ranges / market data] for my role, and I understand the range for roles like mine is [range]. My pay is below that, and over this year I've [receipt 1] and [receipt 2]. I'm asking to move to [number]."
>
> ⏸ **Hush.**

**Notice what's missing:** the colleague's name and salary. Make the case on the market and your work. It's stronger, and it doesn't drag anyone else into it.

**If they say… → You say…**

| They say | You say |
|---|---|
| "Who told you that?" | "I'd rather keep this about my role and the market data. Here's what I've found." |
| "New hires cost more; it's the market." | "That's exactly my point. The market for this role is [range]. I'd like my pay to be in it too." |
| "We can't discuss other people's pay." | "Of course. I'm only asking about mine." |

**If you think the gap is about sex, race, age, disability or another protected characteristic:** keep notes, and talk to a free official service before anything else: EEOC (US), Acas or the Equality Advisory Support Service (UK), your provincial human rights office (Canada), Fair Work Ombudsman (Australia).

## Page 34 · The Slip Page (1): what to do when it goes wrong

Something will probably go a bit wrong. That's normal in a conversation this high-stakes. Here's how to put it right, mostly with one email.

**First rule of slipping: a slip is information, not a verdict.** Write down what happened, pick the repair below, and do it within 48 hours.

| What happened | Why it's fixable | The repair |
|---|---|---|
| **I froze and never said the number** | Nothing was decided, so nothing's lost | Email within 48 h: "Thanks for the chat. I didn't get to the main thing I wanted to say, so here it is: based on [receipts] and [range], I'm asking to move to [number]. Could we talk about it [day]?" |
| **I apologised, said "just", or explained the number five times** | They still heard a number | Nothing to repair out loud. In the follow-up email, restate the number once, cleanly. Next time: circle every "sorry" in your script first |
| **I said a range, and they picked the bottom** | You can still ask for a date | Script e for the gap: "Thank you. Could we agree a review on [date] to get to [top of my range]?" |
| **I said yes to their first number on the spot** | You can still ask about the next step | Email: "Thanks again for the increase. I'd like to agree when we'll next review my pay against [range data], and what I'd need to show. Could we set a date?" |
| **I gave my current or past pay at offer stage** | The offer's not signed yet | Shift the anchor to the role: "Having learned more about the role, I've looked at the market for it. Based on [range], I'm asking for [number]." |
| **I cried, or my voice went** | It's a human reaction to a high-stakes conversation | If you want to, a short note: "Thanks for your patience today. I'd still like to pick up the conversation about my pay. Could we find twenty minutes [day]?" Then rehearse more with the audios |
